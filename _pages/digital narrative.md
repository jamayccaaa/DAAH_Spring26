<h3> 1. Claude </h3>

**Reflection on Digital Literacy: A 20-Year-Old’s Perspective**

As a 20-year-old university student double majoring in Art and Art History and Interactive Media, digital literacy is not just a skillset—it’s a daily necessity and a lens through which I interpret both my academic and personal worlds. Navigating between creative expression and technological fluency, I find myself constantly adapting to new platforms, tools, and collaborative environments. My journey in digital literacy is shaped by the unique intersection of my majors, but also by the diverse skills and perspectives of my peers from fields as varied as Computer Science, Film and New Media, Environmental Studies, Engineering, and Literature.

**Strategies and Tools for Content Creation and Social Interaction**

In my daily life, content creation is second nature. I use Adobe Creative Suite—especially Photoshop, Illustrator, and Premiere Pro—for everything from designing posters for campus events to editing short films for class projects. For interactive assignments, I rely on platforms like Figma for prototyping and Unity for building simple digital experiences. Social media platforms such as Instagram and TikTok are not just for personal expression but also serve as spaces to share my art, connect with other creatives, and even participate in digital art challenges that push my skills further.

Collaboration is a core part of my academic experience. Google Workspace tools (Docs, Slides, Drive) are essential for group projects, allowing real-time editing and seamless communication. For more complex collaborations, especially in Interactive Media, I use GitHub to manage code and version control, even if my coding skills are still developing. Discord and Slack channels keep me connected with classmates, enabling quick feedback and fostering a sense of community beyond the classroom.

**Research, Data Management, and Analysis**

Research in Art History often means managing large sets of images, bibliographies, and notes. I use Zotero for reference management and Evernote for organizing research materials. For data manipulation and analysis, especially in Interactive Media, I am learning to use Python (with libraries like Pandas and Matplotlib) to analyze datasets—whether it’s tracking trends in digital art or visualizing user engagement in interactive installations. Tableau and RAWGraphs help me turn raw data into compelling visual stories, making complex information accessible and engaging.

**What There Is Left to Learn**

Despite these accomplishments, I recognize that digital humanities is a rapidly evolving field with much more to offer. One area I am eager to explore further is computational methods for humanities research. While I have dabbled in Python for basic data manipulation, I want to deepen my understanding of programming so that I can write custom scripts for more advanced text analysis, data cleaning, and visualization. Learning languages like Python and R would open up new possibilities for automating tasks and handling larger datasets.

I am also interested in learning more about digital archives and metadata. Understanding how digital collections are curated, described, and preserved is crucial for ensuring the longevity and accessibility of cultural heritage materials. I would like to gain hands-on experience with tools like Omeka and CONTENTdm, as well as learn about metadata standards such as Dublin Core and TEI. This knowledge would enable me to contribute to the creation and maintenance of digital archives that serve both scholarly and public audiences.

Another area for growth is critical engagement with digital tools and methods. As digital humanities scholars, it is important not only to use technology but also to reflect on its implications. I want to learn more about the ethical considerations involved in digital research, such as issues of privacy, representation, and bias. Engaging with scholarship on digital ethics and participating in discussions about best practices will help me become a more responsible and thoughtful practitioner.

Finally, I am excited to explore emerging technologies such as machine learning, augmented reality, and virtual reality. These tools have the potential to transform how we interact with historical materials and tell stories about the past. I am particularly interested in how immersive experiences can be used to engage new audiences and foster empathy and understanding.

**Conclusion**

In summary, my journey in digital humanities has equipped me with valuable skills in text analysis, data visualization, digital publishing, and collaboration. However, I am aware that there is always more to learn. By deepening my technical expertise, engaging critically with digital methods, and exploring new technologies, I hope to contribute meaningfully to the field and continue to grow as a digital humanist. The possibilities are vast, and I am excited to see where this journey will take me next.

# PART 3

My grasp of digital literacy grew as I learned about different platforms. I initially thought digital literacy was just about using social media and digital platforms while also involved safely and responsibly processing information from the online world. It was my first time exploring computational tools, data analysis, with AI. I found it overwhelming at first as I usually take a while to grasp new software and digital platforms. In class, I analyzed data, engaged with AI, and experimented with tools like geocoding and Voyant Tools. Although I faced some frustration and confusion while learning new processes, I still started to enjoy the journey. It helped me explore fresh ways of thinking, creating, and understanding information. These experiences showed me that digital literacy goes beyond using technology. It also means being adaptable and understanding how digital systems shape how we interpret, organize, and share information in ever-changing environments.

**Platforms Used**

Over the course of the semester, I learned how to use multiple digital tools and platforms such as Visual Studio Code, Voyant Tools, OpenStreetMap, and Hemingway Editor. These are the platforms that stood out to me the most because they were the ones I kept using repeatedly throughout the course. I mainly used VS Code for updating and editing my course website, and honestly, one of the things I enjoyed the most was uploading everything to GitHub Desktop, committing the changes, and then seeing the updates afterward. It made the process feel rewarding, especially after spending time fixing coding mistakes, although there were also moments when it became overwhelming depending on how much coding  or information there was. I also liked using Voyant Tools because I found it interesting to upload different corpora and analyze which words appeared the most. The visualizations and graphs made the data easier to understand, although there were times when it included random or unimportant words that did not really reflect the story itself, which made me realize that computational tools also have limitations.

I also enjoyed using OpenStreetMap because, as someone who frequently uses digital maps, I liked the idea of a platform where users could freely edit and contribute information themselves. At the same time, I realized that because it relies heavily on user contributions, there can also be inconsistencies or missing information depending on the area being viewed. Lastly, although Hemingway Editor was not directly taught in class, I discovered it through one of the assignment instructions and ended up using it often when revising my work. I found it really helpful in identifying sentences that were difficult to read, weakeners, and other writing issues that I usually would not notice immediately. However, one downside was that some features were limited unless you had access to a paid account. Using these different platforms made me realize that digital tools are not completely neutral because they shape the way users organize information, interpret data, and approach different kinds of problems and creative work.

**What I Learned and What I Need to Improve**

One thing I realized throughout the semester was that I was actually good at navigating and adapting to unfamiliar digital interfaces, especially when the platforms felt organized and easier to explore. For example, I found platforms like Voyant Tools easier to adjust to because of their visual and interactive layout. At the same time, platforms such as Posit Cloud and Visual Studio Code initially felt intimidating at first because I was unfamiliar with coding environments and technical interfaces. However, after repeatedly using them throughout the semester, I slowly became more comfortable navigating them. One of the most significant things I learned from the course was being exposed to different digital and computational tools. Before taking the course, I had never heard of most of these platforms, and now I can see myself using some of them again in future classes and projects because of how useful they are for organizing, visualizing, and analyzing information.

At the same time, there are still many areas where I need improvement. I still struggle with coding, especially when debugging and figuring out why small errors can affect an entire project. I also need more practice in deeply analyzing and interpreting data instead of only understanding the visual or surface-level results. Since this was my first time working extensively with computational tools and digital analysis, there were moments when the technical processes became confusing and overwhelming. Despite these challenges, I think the experience helped me become more patient, adaptable, and open to learning technologies that initially seemed difficult or intimidating.

Another important thing I learned throughout the semester was that digital outputs are not always completely accurate. Through activities like using Photogrammar one time, I realized that data can still be limited depending on what information is included in a dataset. Since Photogrammar mainly focuses on photographs from the United States, it made me wonder about the places and perspectives that were not represented in the archive. I also became more aware of how easily digital information can be changed online. During one of our activities using OpenStreetMap, we changed the name of the school cafe from Starbucks to the cafe that currently exists today, which showed me how easily users can edit information on collaborative platforms. Because AI and other digital systems often gather information from online datasets and sources, inaccurate information can also affect the outputs they generate. This made me realize that even though AI and digital tools are useful, people still need to verify whether the information is accurate since algorithms can sometimes misinterpret context or meaning. Because of this course, I became more critical and questioning when using digital tools and online information instead of automatically trusting digital outputs immediately.

**Working with AI**

Working with AI throughout the semester has been beneficial for me. I believe that as long as we use it responsibly and for the right reasons, it can be a helpful tool in our work instead of something that completely replaces human effort. I found AI and other computational tools useful because they can save time, explain unfamiliar concepts, assist with figuring out what's next, and help organize information more efficiently. For example, during our geocoding assignment about Southern Arabia, I used Posit Cloud to help generate an automated geocoding from the text. Although Posit Cloud itself is not exactly an AI, it is still a useful platform for coding, data analysis, and computational work. When I compared the manually geocoded map with the automated version, I noticed several differences between them. The automated geocoding process was much faster and more efficient, but there were also incorrect placenames and locations on the map. This made me realize that even though computational tools can process information quickly, human interpretation is still important because people understand the context and meaning of a story more deeply than algorithms or datasets alone. This connects to what Drucker discusses in Digital Humanities Overview, where she explains that AI and digital systems can still contain biases depending on the data they are trained on.

For the digital narrative assignment, I realized that AI is becoming very good at writing in terms of fluency and structure. However, I also noticed that if people rely too heavily on AI-generated writing, the responses can start to sound repetitive, generic, and lacking in personal experience or emotion. Sometimes AI follows almost the same flow or wording for different prompts, as seen from the prompts made during the part 1, which can make the writing feel less natural and personal. There is also the possibility of inaccurate information if the output is not checked carefully. Because of this, I believe that AI works best when it assists people instead of replacing them completely. AI writing has improved a lot, but good writing still needs human insight, judgment, creativity, and real-life experience.

**Reflection**

Because of this course and the lessons that I learned during the semester, I became more critical and questioning when using digital tools and online information. Instead of automatically trusting digital outputs, I now pay more attention to where the data comes from and whether the source itself is reliable, especially on websites that can easily be edited by users such as Wikipedia. This connects to what Drucker discusses in Digital Humanities Overview, where she explains that digital work is not only about using tools to make tasks easier or faster, but also about understanding the systems and structures behind them. Throughout the semester, I realized that digital literacy is not just about learning how to use platforms on a surface level, but also about critically understanding how information is collected, interpreted, organized, and presented online. Overall, the course changed the way I think about technology, data, and AI, and it made me more aware of both the possibilities and limitations of digital systems.
